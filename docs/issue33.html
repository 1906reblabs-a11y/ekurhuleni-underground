EKURHULENIUNDERGROUND

The hidden systems of the East Rand

Theme Analysis — Issue 33

The Ekurhuleni Kraal Economy

Why this theme matters for the long-term economic story of Ekurhuleni

The theme of the Ekurhuleni Kraal Economy is not a story about livestock. It is a story about an obligation the formal economy has never learned to serve: the requirement that a wedding, a funeral, an initiation, or an ancestral rite have an animal at its centre, sourced, held, and slaughtered on a timeline that answers to culture, not to a municipal calendar.

The deep story: how cattle and goats move from Mpumalanga, the Free State, and KwaZulu-Natal along corridors this publication has already traced for produce and coal, into holding grounds and backyard kraals across Ekurhuleni's older townships and agricultural fringes — sold, kept, and slaughtered by traders, herders, and permit-fluent facilitators operating alongside, rather than inside, the by-law framework the municipality built nearly two decades ago to govern them.

Three supporting micro-stories: the livestock traders who bring cattle and goats into the East Rand along the region's rural-to-urban supply routes, selling directly to families rather than through any formal auction; the herders who hold animals on informal grazing land at the township fringe, turning marginal scrubland into a rolling weekend inventory; and the permit-fluent facilitators who help households navigate, or quietly work around, a council notification process built for a fourteen-day world that grief and ceremony rarely respect.

The overlooked place: the scrubland tract between KwaThema and Springs, where kraals have grazed cattle and goats for East Rand ceremonies for decades — documented occasionally, counted never.

The interesting statistic: by the national Department of Agriculture's own account, presented to Parliament in December 2025, an estimated 0.05% of South Africa's goat herd is slaughtered through a licensed abattoir. The rest moves through cash, live-animal transactions no register captures — including the East Rand's own weekend demand.

Weekly Issue — 4 September 2026

The Weekend Herd

How cattle and goats move from Mpumalanga, the Free State, and KwaZulu-Natal into East Rand kraals and backyard holding grounds — and why the by-law meant to govern their slaughter was written for a rhythm that grief and ceremony have never kept.

1. Editorial
What Grazes in the Gap

In the scrubland that runs between KwaThema and Springs — land too marginal for development, too close to the rail reserve and the workings of a mining era that ended decades ago to interest anyone with a title deed — a man moves through waist-high grass most mornings with a stick and a whistle, counting cattle and goats that belong, individually, to households scattered across three or four townships. He knows which cow is due to calve, which goat has been promised to a family in Vosloorus for a wedding in three weeks, which steer's owner has not paid this month's grazing fee and needs a reminder. None of it is written down. All of it is remembered, animal by animal, the way a shopkeeper remembers a regular's order.

I have been circling this scrubland, and others like it across Ekurhuleni's older townships, for some months — drawn by an observation that seemed, at first, almost too obvious to build an issue around: that South Africans buy and slaughter animals for ceremonial reasons every weekend, that this has been true for as long as anyone can remember, and that almost nothing about how the animals get here — who sources them, who holds them, who navigates the permit meant to govern their slaughter — has ever been written down anywhere a planner or an economist would think to look.

The City of Ekurhuleni has, since 2009, had a public health by-law that explicitly permits ritual and religious slaughter, provided a household gives fourteen days' written notice and secures council permission. It is a reasonable rule, built to balance a constitutionally protected practice against the concerns of neighbours. It is also a rule that assumes grief, celebration, and ancestral obligation can be scheduled two weeks in advance — an assumption the animals moving through KwaThema's scrubland, and the traders and herders who supply them, have never quite been able to accommodate.

This issue follows that gap: the corridor bringing cattle and goats into the East Rand, the kraals that hold them, and the people who help a household get an animal from ground to backyard, on a timeline the by-law was not built to match.

2. The Long Essay
The Herd That Crossed the Line
Section I — The Observation

Walk the access track into the scrubland between KwaThema and Springs on a weekday morning and the kraal economy announces itself before it is visible — the low complaint of cattle, the higher bleat of goats, a dog's bark pitched to move a straggler back toward the herd. The kraal itself is unremarkable: a rough enclosure of salvaged fencing and cut branches, no different from one you might find three hundred kilometres away in a rural district, except that it sits within sight of a rail signal and a cellphone tower, and the traffic carrying commuters into Springs is a constant, low undertone. This incongruity — livestock husbandry metres from suburban infrastructure — is not a curiosity. It is a functioning supply chain's storage facility, doing for cattle and goats what a warehouse does for any commodity awaiting a buyer.

Section II — The Hidden System

The kraal economy of the East Rand operates across three layers, each solving a problem neither the formal livestock industry nor the municipality's by-law framework was built to address.

The first is sourcing. Cattle and goats reach Ekurhuleni's townships along the same eastern and northern corridors this publication has traced moving produce, coal, and cross-border goods — trucks bringing animals from Mpumalanga's communal grazing districts, the Free State, and, for specific ceremonial breeds and colours, further afield. Traders sell directly to households and to the herders who will hold the animals afterward, at a rate that has, by industry accounts, consistently exceeded the formal abattoir price — because the buyer is not purchasing meat. He is purchasing a specific animal, for a specific rite, that the formal system has no product to substitute.

The second layer is holding. On grazing tracts at the township fringe — land too marginal, too close to rail and mine infrastructure, for formal development — herders keep cattle and goats between purchase and slaughter on behalf of owners with neither the space nor the daily availability to do it themselves. The herder's knowledge is entirely relational: which animal belongs to which household, which is promised for which date, whose account is current. He is paid a modest monthly fee per animal, structured less like wages than a storage rental with a living product.

The third layer is slaughter-day facilitation. Ekurhuleni's 2009 by-laws permit ritual slaughter but require fourteen days' written notice and council permission — a timeline that assumes a household can schedule a rite the way it might schedule a delivery. Into the gap between that requirement and the often urgent timing of grief or celebration, a layer of facilitators has moved: people who know which documentation the environmental health office will accept, who advise on the screening and hygiene the by-law requires, and who, for households the two-week window does not serve, quietly manage the slaughter without it.

The by-law does not forbid the rite. It simply assumes that grief, like a delivery, can be scheduled two weeks in advance.
Section III — The Broader Insight

What the kraal economy reveals about Ekurhuleni is a variation on a pattern this publication has traced across water, credit, health transport, and funeral logistics: a formal instrument — a by-law explicitly designed to accommodate a constitutionally protected practice — that nonetheless assumes a rhythm of life the practice itself does not follow. The 2009 by-law is not hostile to ritual slaughter; it is permissive. But its fourteen-day notice period was written for an administrative world, not a ceremonial one, and the gap has produced exactly the kind of informal infrastructure this publication keeps finding: traders, herders, and facilitators who do, reliably and at scale, what the formal system technically allows but practically cannot accommodate on the timeline that matters.

This matters for how Ekurhuleni understands its own economic base because the kraal economy is not a residual activity at the municipality's edges. It is the supply chain behind an obligation a majority of East Rand households will, at some point, need to meet — and it moves real value: live animals, transported, held, and sold across a network no agricultural census, economic profile, or livestock register has ever thought to price. The animals are counted, if at all, only in the moment a family gives notice. Everything upstream happens in a statistical shadow the size of the practice itself.

Section IV — The Antifragile Angle

The kraal economy strengthens, rather than merely persists, whenever the formal channels around it tighten. When feed cost inflation or rand depreciation pushes up the price of meat through formal retail, the live-animal market — priced directly between buyer and seller, outside any abattoir's cost structure — becomes relatively more attractive, and traders report steadier demand. When the municipality's notification process becomes stricter or slower — as residents in several townships, including Katlehong and Vosloorus, have recently petitioned against, arguing the fourteen-day rule ignores the urgency grief and ancestral obligation impose — the facilitators who help households navigate or bypass that timeline become correspondingly more necessary. And when national efforts to formalise the goat and cattle trade advance, as the Department of Agriculture's December 2025 proposal to Parliament intends, the reform is built around breeding programmes, mobile abattoirs, and retail negotiation aimed at commercial producers — not at the household buying one goat for a wedding, whose business stays exactly where it already is.

3. The Hidden Economy
Three Operations Nobody Is Watching
1. The Corridor Traders
Tsakane / Eastern Approach Roads

Along the eastern approaches into Tsakane, where the road corridor this publication has already traced for produce and coal also carries a livestock trade, traders bring cattle and goats from Mpumalanga and the Free State directly to buyers, bypassing any formal auction. They sell on relationship and reputation — a trader whose animals arrive healthy and correctly typed for a specific rite builds a client list spanning several townships. Their prices track the informal live-market rate, which by industry accounts consistently exceeds the formal abattoir price, because the buyer is purchasing a rite, not a carcass.

2. The Kraal Keepers
KwaThema / Springs Scrubland Boundary

On the grazing tract between KwaThema and Springs — marginal land too close to rail and old mine workings for formal development — herders hold cattle and goats for township households who have neither the space nor the daily time to keep animals themselves. Each herder tracks his charges entirely from memory: which animal belongs to which family, which is promised for which date, whose grazing account is overdue. He is paid a modest monthly fee per head — a storage rental for a living inventory that has held East Rand ceremonies to their calendar for decades.

3. The Permit Runners
Katlehong

In Katlehong, where residents have petitioned the municipality over a fourteen-day notice requirement many say ignores the urgency of grief and ceremony, a small number of facilitators help households navigate the ritual slaughter by-law — assembling the paperwork the environmental health office requires, advising on the screening and hygiene the law demands, or, for families the window cannot serve, quietly managing a slaughter without it. Their fee is modest; their standing is entirely reputational, built one household at a time.

4. The Map of the Week
The KwaThema–Springs Grazing Tract
26°16′S  28°25′E

A few hectares of scrubland, no more. That is the working footprint of the grazing tract between KwaThema and Springs — land bypassed by formal development because it sits too close to a rail reserve and old mine workings, and precisely for that reason, land that has functioned as an informal kraal for East Rand ceremonies for as long as anyone herding there can remember. In that scrubland you will find rough enclosures built from salvaged fencing and cut branches, herders who know each animal individually, and a rhythm of movement timed not to any season but to the East Rand's weekend calendar of weddings, funerals, and rites.

What you will not find is a single sign, register, or municipal marker acknowledging any of it. To stand at the tract's edge on a Saturday morning, watching a bakkie load a goat for a wedding forty minutes away, is to understand Ekurhuleni's most reliable weekly commodity market has no address.

5. The East Rand Number
0.05% Estimated share of South Africa's national goat herd slaughtered through a licensed abattoir — the rest moving through cash transactions no municipal register captures, East Rand ceremonies included

By the national Department of Agriculture's own account, presented to Parliament in December 2025, an estimated 0.05% of South Africa's goat herd is slaughtered through a licensed abattoir. The Department's own value-chain research puts the commercial-sector share slightly higher, at 0.55%. Either figure describes the same reality: most of the country's goats move through cash, live-animal transactions — a category the East Rand's weekend ceremonial demand sits inside, uncounted, every Saturday.

6. Closing Reflection
The Field Between the Fences

The herder in the scrubland between KwaThema and Springs will be there again this Saturday, and the one after, moving through grass no development plan has claimed and no agricultural census has counted, matching animals to households whose ceremonies do not consult a municipal calendar. He does not think of himself as running a supply chain. He is simply keeping cattle and goats that belong, for now, to people who will need them on a date grief or custom has already set. The question the kraal economy leaves behind is not whether Ekurhuleni will ever register this trade. It is whether a register, built for a different rhythm, could ever hold it.
