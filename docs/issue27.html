EKURHULENIUNDERGROUND

The hidden systems of the East Rand

Theme Analysis — Issue 27

The Muthi Corridor

Why this theme matters for the long-term economic story of Ekurhuleni

The theme of the Muthi Corridor is not a story about traditional healing. It is a story about a supply chain — one that moves dried bark, root, and herb material by the tonne from harvesters in KwaZulu-Natal and Mpumalanga into two Johannesburg wholesale markets and onward into the fixed shops and street traders of Ekurhuleni's townships, sustaining an economy larger than most this publication has measured.

The deep story: how a national indigenous medicine trade valued at approximately R2.9 billion a year — and, once broader herbal and natural-product retail is included, plausibly several times that — moves through Ekurhuleni not as a producer but as a consumption and distribution node, absorbed into township shops and itinerant trading networks that have operated for decades without appearing in a single municipal economic profile, and without the practitioners who anchor the trade holding any form of state-recognised professional registration nearly two decades after Parliament passed the law meant to give them one.

Three supporting micro-stories: the muthi shopkeepers of Wattville, south of Benoni, whose stock arrives through kinship and trust networks reaching back to the KwaZulu-Natal households many of their families migrated from; the bulk-breaking traders of Vosloorus who buy consolidated consignments from Johannesburg's Faraday and Kwa Mai-Mai muthi markets and repackage them into the smaller lots township retailers can afford; and the administrative fixers operating near Tembisa's health precinct who help traditional healers navigate a professional registration system that, two decades after enabling legislation, still has no functioning public register.

The overlooked place: the muthi shop cluster along Abie Nyalunga Road and Thamie Street in Wattville — a dense, established township whose Zulu-speaking majority has sustained a direct commercial and kinship line to the trade's principal source province since before Ekurhuleni existed as a municipality.

The interesting statistic: South Africans consume an estimated 70,000 tonnes of medicinal plant material annually, worth approximately R2.9 billion by the most rigorous available accounting — a trade larger than most sectors this publication has measured, and one no Ekurhuleni economic development document has ever named.

Weekly Issue — 24 July 2026

The Second Pharmacy

How Ekurhuleni's township muthi shops anchor the retail end of a national supply chain moving bark, root, and remedy from KwaZulu-Natal and Mpumalanga harvesters into the East Rand — and why South Africa's second-largest health system has never appeared on a single municipal economic plan.

1. Editorial
What Arrives Wrapped in Newspaper

A shop on Abie Nyalunga Road in Wattville opens before seven most mornings, shutters lifted onto a narrow room lined floor to ceiling with sacks, bundles, and glass jars whose contents carry no printed label. The owner spends the first hour of most days unwrapping what arrived overnight: root bound in twine, bark broken into fist-sized pieces and packed in old newspaper, a plastic bag of dried leaf tied at the neck with string. None of it travelled by courier. All of it has a name, in several languages, depending on who is asking.

I have been curious about shops like this one for some time, not because their existence is a secret — muthi shops are a familiar fixture of township commercial streets across South Africa — but because almost nothing about how they are supplied has ever been written down with any precision. The stock did not grow in Wattville. Most of it did not grow anywhere near Ekurhuleni. It travelled, by long-distance taxi and bus, from harvesters and growers in KwaZulu-Natal and Mpumalanga, through two wholesale markets in central Johannesburg that most Ekurhuleni residents have never visited, and into a shop like this one through relationships older than the municipality that now contains it.

This publication has spent twenty-six issues describing hidden supply chains — scrap metal, secondhand clothing, chemical inputs, freight moving on the return leg of a taxi route. The muthi trade belongs in that company for a specific reason: it is not small. By the most careful academic accounting available, South Africans consume an estimated 70,000 tonnes of medicinal plant material a year, worth approximately R2.9 billion, sustaining roughly 133,000 livelihoods nationally. Some 72% of the population uses traditional medicine at some point, often alongside, not instead of, conventional healthcare. That makes it, by any reasonable measure, the country's second-largest health system. It is also a system whose practitioners have waited eighteen years for a professional register that still, as of this writing, does not functionally exist.

This issue traces that system as it actually operates across the East Rand: not where the plants are grown, and not where the biggest wholesale markets sit, but in the township shops, the bulk-breaking traders, and the administrative fixers who move consignments, currency, and paperwork through a distribution layer that has never once appeared in an Ekurhuleni economic profile. None of it is exotic. It is, in the precise sense this publication keeps returning to, infrastructure — for a form of primary healthcare that a majority of the region's residents already use, and that the region has never thought to count.

2. The Long Essay
The Trade That Predates the Municipality
Section I — The Observation

Walk the commercial stretch of Abie Nyalunga Road on a weekday morning and the muthi shops announce themselves quietly, wedged between a hair salon and a cash-and-carry, distinguishable mainly by smell: a mineral, slightly bitter note that mixes dried bark, root powder, and burnt impepho. Inside, stock is organised by a logic that has nothing to do with alphabetical order and everything to do with use — remedies grouped by ailment, protective mixtures kept apart from purgatives, animal-derived material, where it is stocked at all, kept furthest from the door. The shopkeeper knows every item by more than one name, because a customer from KwaZulu-Natal, a customer from Mpumalanga, and a customer born in Ekurhuleni to parents from either province will each ask for the same root differently. None of this inventory arrived through a supplier with a delivery schedule. It arrived because someone, at some point, built a relationship that has been maintained, consignment by consignment, for years.

Section II — The Hidden System

The muthi economy of the East Rand operates across three layers, each a documented feature of South Africa's traditional medicine trade more broadly, each largely invisible from outside the trade itself.

The first layer is harvesting and primary supply, based almost entirely outside Ekurhuleni. Academic surveys of the trade consistently find that the great majority of traders at South Africa's major muthi markets are migrants from KwaZulu-Natal, and that the bulk of bark and root material moving through the country's urban markets originates in the province's rural districts, with Mpumalanga's Lowveld a significant secondary source. Harvesters — increasingly travelling further from home as easily accessible wild populations of the most sought-after species have thinned — sell to consolidators who bulk material for transport to the country's largest wholesale points.

The second layer is wholesale distribution, concentrated at two markets in central Johannesburg: the Faraday Muthi Market and the Kwa Mai-Mai Market, both long-established, both functioning as the clearing houses through which the majority of Gauteng's traditional medicine trade passes. Traders here — surveys put the workforce at roughly two-thirds female, and the great majority KwaZulu-Natal-born — buy in volume from consolidators and sell in smaller lots to secondary traders, including the East Rand shopkeepers and bulk-breakers who rarely need to visit the market themselves, because a relationship, once established, is maintained by phone and by the same taxi routes that move passengers.

The third layer is East Rand retail and distribution — the layer this publication can actually observe. In Wattville, Vosloorus, and townships across Ekurhuleni, fixed muthi shops and itinerant traders receive consignments broken down from the Johannesburg wholesale volume, priced for township purchasing power, and sold alongside consultation services from resident or visiting traditional healers. The shopkeeper is rarely just a retailer. In many cases the same person diagnosing a client's ailment is also the one who sourced, priced, and stocked the remedy — a vertical integration of diagnosis and supply that no pharmacy licensing framework in the country currently recognises as a single regulated function.

Dried bark and root do not need a cold chain, an import licence, or a functioning hospital pharmacy to keep working.
Section III — The Broader Insight

What the muthi corridor reveals about Ekurhuleni is a variation on a pattern this publication has traced through scrap metal, secondhand clothing, and cross-border produce: an economy of real scale operating on relationships of kinship and trust that long predate the administrative geography it now sits inside. The townships that anchor the East Rand's muthi retail — Wattville among them — were built, in most cases, to house a labour force drawn substantially from KwaZulu-Natal during decades of mine and industrial recruitment. The commercial relationships that now move root and bark from Durban's hinterland to a shop on Abie Nyalunga Road did not have to be built from scratch. They were already there, carried by the same families whose labour built the East Rand's mines and factories, repurposed for a different kind of trade once the original one declined.

This matters for how the municipality thinks about its own economic base. A sector serving a majority of Ekurhuleni's residents, moving a documented multi-billion-rand national trade, and providing livelihoods to shopkeepers, bulk-breakers, and healers across every township this publication has profiled, does not appear in a single municipal economic development document. It is not that the trade is hidden from the people who use it. It is that the instruments used to describe Ekurhuleni's economy were never built to see something organised this way.

Section IV — The Antifragile Angle

The muthi trade has a structural relationship to the specific pressures that damage its formal healthcare counterpart. When South Africa's public clinics face medicine stockouts and staffing shortfalls — a documented, recurring feature of the provincial health system — patients whose chronic or culturally understood conditions require regular treatment do not stop seeking care. Many turn, or return, to a traditional healer whose supply chain does not depend on a cold chain, an import licence, or a functioning hospital pharmacy. Dried bark and root do not spoil without refrigeration. They require no import permit, because the material never crosses a formal border inspection point in commercial volume. They are priced in cash, at a scale a shopkeeper controls directly, insulated from the currency-driven cost spikes that periodically strain the formal pharmaceutical supply chain.

This is not resilience in the passive sense. Each stockout, each queue, each rand depreciation that makes an imported medicine more expensive relative to a domestically sourced remedy widens the price and reliability gap in the muthi trade's favour. The shopkeeper on Abie Nyalunga Road does not need the formal health system to fail in order to have customers. But when it does, her supply chain — off-grid, cash-based, immune to cold-chain and currency exposure alike — is the one still open the next morning.

3. The Hidden Economy
Three Operations Nobody Is Watching
1. The Root Sellers
Wattville, Benoni

Along Abie Nyalunga Road and the side streets running off it, a cluster of fixed muthi shops trades bark, root, and prepared remedies sourced almost entirely through relationships their owners describe simply as family. Many shopkeepers can trace their stock back to specific harvesting households in rural KwaZulu-Natal — the same province the township's Zulu-speaking majority largely migrated from during decades of East Rand mine and industrial recruitment. Consignments arrive by long-distance taxi, paid for by mobile money before departure and settled in cash on arrival. No supplier contract exists. The relationship is the supply chain, maintained across several hundred kilometres by trust that predates the shop itself.

2. The Bulk-Breakers
Mohlopi Street, Vosloorus

A smaller number of traders in Vosloorus perform a function the shopkeepers of the wider East Rand depend on without necessarily naming it: buying consolidated consignments from Johannesburg's Faraday and Kwa Mai-Mai muthi markets in volumes no single township shop could individually justify, then dividing them into the smaller lots retailers can actually afford. Their margin is the price gap between wholesale and township-retail volume; their knowledge is the ability to judge which bark grades and species will move fastest in which township that week. They hold no formal distribution agreement with either Johannesburg market. Their standing rests entirely on years of reliable, on-time buying.

3. The Registration Brokers
Tembisa Health Precinct

Near Tembisa's hospital and clinic precinct, a small number of informal fixers have built a modest business helping traditional healers navigate a registration system that, eighteen years after the enabling Act was passed, still has no fully functioning public register. They assist healers in assembling the community testimonials, training records, and category documentation the interim council's draft process requires, translate bureaucratic language into practical steps, and follow up on applications that can otherwise disappear for months. They are not registered themselves. Their expertise is procedural rather than medical — fluency in a bureaucracy that the people it was built to serve still cannot reliably navigate alone.

4. The Map of the Week
The Abie Nyalunga Road Muthi Cluster, Wattville
26°13′S  28°18′E

A few hundred metres. That is the working length of the muthi shop cluster along Abie Nyalunga Road and the side streets feeding into it, in a township established in 1941 to house African workers displaced from freehold land closer to Benoni's centre. In that stretch you will find: fixed shops with hand-painted signage naming specific ailments rather than brands; the smell of dried bark and root before any shop is visible; consultation rooms at the back of several premises where a resident healer sees clients by appointment; and, most mornings, shopkeepers unpacking newspaper-wrapped bundles on the pavement before the day's first customer arrives.

What you will not find is any signage from a municipal economic development office, any listing in a commercial property register, or any official indication that this cluster is trade at all. To walk Abie Nyalunga Road at seven, watching bark arrive from a province three hundred kilometres away, is to understand that Ekurhuleni's second-largest health system has operated in plain sight for longer than the municipality has existed.

5. The East Rand Number
~R2.9bn Estimated annual value of South Africa's traditional medicine trade — a figure nearly two decades old, widely cited as the most rigorous available, and almost certainly a conservative account of what moves through township shops like those on Abie Nyalunga Road today

The most careful economic accounting of South Africa's traditional medicine trade — research dating to 2006 and 2007, still the most rigorous estimate available — puts its annual value at approximately R2.9 billion, generated from roughly 70,000 tonnes of plant material and sustaining an estimated 133,000 livelihoods nationally. No comparable national study has been conducted since. Industry observers argue the true figure, inflated by two decades of urbanisation and currency change, runs considerably higher. Either way, no Ekurhuleni economic profile has ever named a rand value for the trade moving through its own townships.

6. Closing Reflection
What the Shop Already Knew

The shopkeeper on Abie Nyalunga Road did not wait for the Traditional Health Practitioners Act to be fully enacted before opening her doors, and she will not close them if the interim council's register remains incomplete for another decade. Her business does not depend on the state's recognition. It depends on a relationship with a harvesting household in KwaZulu-Natal that has outlasted several changes of government, and on customers who have never needed a municipal economic profile to tell them where to find a remedy. The question worth asking is not whether Ekurhuleni will eventually count this trade. It is what else, in a city this large, is already this consequential, and still waiting to be seen.
