The hidden systems of the East Rand

Theme Analysis — Issue 30

The Tembisa Eldercare Circuit

Why this theme matters for the long-term economic story of Ekurhuleni

The theme of the Tembisa Eldercare Circuit is not a story about growing old. It is a story about who shows up at seven in the evening when the formal system's shift has ended — a cash-paid, reputation-governed network of night carers, patient-transport drivers, and backyard frail-care operators who have built an eldercare economy sized to actual demand, inside a township the formal subsidy budget was never resized to match.

The deep story: how Tembisa's informal eldercare sector — night-duty home carers hired directly by families, bakkie operators running subscription clinic and dialysis shuttles, and unregistered frail-care houses converted from ordinary rooms — has absorbed a share of South Africa's fastest-growing demographic cohort that the country's 417 registered residential facilities and DSD home-based care programmes were never resourced to serve, as the national 60-plus population grew from 3.6 million in 2002 to 6.6 million in 2025 with no comparable growth in formal capacity.

Three supporting micro-stories: the night sitters of Hospital View, arranged entirely through family referral and paid directly in cash; the clinic-run drivers of Winnie Mandela Park, who run subscription patient transport for dialysis and chronic-medication appointments that ambulances do not reliably cover; and the backyard frail-care operators of the Tembisa–Clayville fringe, housing a handful of elderly residents at a fraction of registered-facility cost, absorbing overflow from a waiting list with no realistic path to clearing.

The overlooked place: Hospital View, the Tembisa section named for its proximity to Tembisa Hospital, where the township's informal night-care economy is most visibly concentrated around shift change, discharge, and the daily walk to the clinic gate.

The interesting statistic: Gauteng recorded approximately 1.4 million residents aged 60 and older in 2022 — the largest elderly population, by absolute number, of any province — against a national aged-care system built, in institutional memory if not policy, around the assumption that ageing is principally a rural, Eastern Cape story.

Weekly Issue — 14 August 2026

The Night Nurses

How an unregistered network of night-duty carers, clinic-run drivers, and backyard frail-care operators in Tembisa built Ekurhuleni's largest eldercare system — one the formal welfare and health sector was never resourced to match.

1. Editorial
The Hours Nobody Bills For

At quarter to seven most evenings, a woman turns off Hospital View's main road in Tembisa and walks the last two blocks to a house she has visited nightly for eleven months. She lets herself in with a key given in the third week, greets whichever family member is leaving for a night shift or going to bed, and settles into a chair beside a bed where an eighty-one-year-old stroke patient will need turning every two hours until morning. She makes tea before dawn, changes a sheet without waking anyone else, and leaves at six, in time for the taxi rank before the morning queue builds. She is paid weekly, in cash, an amount agreed eleven months ago and not renegotiated since. No agency placed her. No inspector has seen the room she works in. By any reasonable definition she is doing nursing work. She has never called herself a nurse, because the word belongs, formally, to someone holding a certificate she does not have.

I have been curious for some time about who actually performs this work across Ekurhuleni's townships, because the answer that South Africa's welfare statistics imply — registered facilities, subsidised home-based care programmes, a state system doing what a state system is meant to do — describes a capacity that has not grown at anything like the rate of the population it is meant to serve. The country's population aged sixty and older very nearly doubled between 2002 and 2025. The number of state-registered facilities to house the frailest of them, and the funded community-care posts to visit the rest at home, did not remotely keep pace. Gauteng, not the rural provinces popular imagination associates with an ageing population, now holds more residents over sixty than any other province in the country by absolute count — a fact that sits uneasily inside a policy conversation still largely framed around rural care deficits.

Into the gap between that growth and that capacity, Tembisa has built an economy: night carers hired directly by families, patient-transport drivers running informal clinic shuttles, and small unregistered frail-care houses absorbing residents the formal waiting list will not reach in time. None of it appears in a municipal health profile. All of it is, on any given evening, keeping someone alive until morning. This issue is an account of how that economy actually works.

2. The Long Essay
The Care the Grant Doesn't Cover
Section I — The Observation

Walk Hospital View in the early evening, when Tembisa Hospital's day shift is changing and the clinic queue outside the gate has thinned to its last stragglers, and the township's second economy becomes legible in a specific kind of foot traffic: women, mostly, moving purposefully toward houses that are not their own, carrying a small bag — a phone charger, a flask, a change of clothes for the morning — that marks them as arriving for the night rather than for a visit. They do not wear uniforms. Nothing distinguishes the house they are walking toward from any other on the street, except that inside it, someone old and frail is waiting for the day's last carer to arrive before the family can, finally, sleep.

Section II — The Hidden System

Tembisa's eldercare economy operates through three layers, each addressing a specific gap in the formal system that state registration and state subsidy have left open.

The first is the night-duty carer, hired directly by a household rather than through any placement agency or Department of Social Development programme. Families who have tried the formal route describe the same obstacle repeatedly: registered home-based care posts are funded at a scale that reaches only a fraction of eligible households, and the community caregivers who do hold them are typically stretched across a daily caseload that leaves little room for the overnight, one-on-one attention a frail or bedridden patient actually needs. Into that gap, households turn to direct hire — a carer found through a neighbour, a church network, or a previous client's referral, paid a negotiated weekly cash amount that both sides understand will adjust informally if the patient's needs change. There is no contract. There is a reputation, built one household at a time, that functions as the only credentialing system either party requires.

The second layer is patient transport. Chronic conditions common among Tembisa's older residents — diabetes, hypertension, the dialysis end-stage kidney disease requires several times a week — demand a regularity of clinic visits that public ambulance services, built around emergency response rather than routine transport, do not reliably provide. A network of bakkie and minibus operators has built subscription shuttle routes instead: a flat weekly fee, a pickup time calibrated to the clinic's actual queue rhythm rather than its official opening hour, and a driver who knows which pharmacy counter dispenses chronic medication fastest on which day of the month. Families who tried coordinating this transport themselves, around jobs that do not pause for a mid-morning dialysis slot, describe the driver's schedule as more dependable than their own.

The third layer is the backyard frail-care house — a converted room, sometimes a small block of rooms, housing three to six elderly residents under an operator who is, typically, an experienced caregiver who began by looking after one relative and expanded as word spread. South Africa's 417 registered residential facilities for older persons, and the many unregistered private facilities alongside them, together fall well short of documented demand; waiting lists at registered facilities routinely run longer than the beds available. Tembisa's backyard operators do not wait for a vacancy. They add a mattress.

A registered facility has a waiting list. A backyard room has a mattress and a door that opens when a family calls.
Section III — The Broader Insight

What Tembisa's eldercare economy reveals is a demographic mismatch that South Africa's welfare policy conversation has been slow to name in urban, industrial terms. Population ageing in South Africa is still discussed, by habit, as a story about the Eastern Cape and rural KwaZulu-Natal — provinces with the highest proportional share of older residents. Gauteng's story is different in kind: a lower proportional share of a much larger population, which nonetheless adds up, in absolute terms, to the country's largest elderly population by province. Ekurhuleni, as one of Gauteng's most populous metros, carries a meaningful share of that number inside townships that municipal and provincial health planning has continued to model primarily around working-age and youth demand. The informal eldercare economy that has grown up in response is not a failure of family responsibility. It is a rational, reputation-governed labour market filling a capacity gap that a frozen subsidy structure was never going to close on its own.

Section IV — The Antifragile Angle

The pressure that expands Tembisa's informal eldercare sector is specific and structural: the widening distance between the size of the registered, subsidised aged-care system and the size of the population it is meant to serve. That system's growth has been additionally constrained by a documented pattern of underfunding — subsidy payments to registered facilities and community-care NGOs that arrive late or fall short, and public-sector wage growth that has, by the welfare department's own researchers' account, absorbed budget that would otherwise have expanded frontline service capacity. Each family turned away from a full registered facility, placed on a home-based care waiting list, or left managing a discharge with no funded follow-up support becomes, immediately, a customer for one of Tembisa's three informal layers. The informal system has no equivalent capacity ceiling. A backyard operator with a spare room adds a resident. A driver with room in the bakkie adds a passenger to the Tuesday dialysis run. A carer with an evening free takes another family's referral. Every constraint that keeps the formal system from expanding is, for the informal one, simply more demand arriving at a door that was already open.

3. The Hidden Economy
Three Operations Nobody Is Watching
1. The Night Sitters
Hospital View, Tembisa

Along the streets nearest Tembisa Hospital, a loose network of women work multi-night shifts sitting with frail or bedridden patients, hired directly by families through referral rather than any agency. They are paid weekly, in cash, at a rate rarely revisited once agreed. Their value is a competence no certificate confirms: turning a stroke patient without pain, reading the early signs of a pressure sore, keeping a household calm through a bad night. Their client base grows through word passed between families who found no formal service that could reliably provide it.

2. The Clinic Runners
Winnie Mandela Park, Tembisa

Operating bakkies and minibuses from the streets nearest Tembisa's main taxi rank and clinic precinct, a cluster of drivers runs subscription patient transport for residents managing dialysis, hypertension, and diabetes — conditions needing regular clinic visits public ambulances are not built to provide. Clients pay a flat weekly or monthly fee, not a per-trip fare. The drivers' knowledge is timing: which queue moves fastest at which hour, which pharmacy counter dispenses chronic medication fastest, and which patients need collecting ten minutes earlier than the family thinks.

3. The Backyard Frail-Care Houses
Tembisa / Clayville Fringe

Along the fringe where Tembisa meets Clayville's older residential stock, a small number of operators run informal frail-care houses from converted rooms, housing three to six elderly residents at a fee well below a registered facility's charge. Most began by caring for a single relative and expanded as neighbouring families, unable to secure a place on a registered waiting list, asked for the same arrangement. No department has inspected these houses. No subsidy funds them. Quality assurance is the same as for the night sitters: a satisfied family telling the next one where to go.

4. The Map of the Week
Hospital View, Tembisa
25°59′S  28°13′E

A few residential blocks. That is the working footprint of Hospital View, the Tembisa section named for its proximity to Tembisa Hospital, where the township's informal eldercare economy is most visibly concentrated. In those blocks you will find: night carers walking toward their evening shift as the hospital's day staff walk the opposite way; family members waiting at the clinic gate for a lift home no formal ambulance service will provide for a routine discharge; and, in the quieter streets further from the fence, the converted rooms where frail-care houses operate without a sign, a licence, or a waiting list.

What you will not find is any street-level sign of which houses provide this care. The economy is legible only through timing — who arrives at seven, who leaves at six, which bakkie idles outside a gate every Tuesday. Ekurhuleni's largest eldercare provider is not a facility. It is a network of people who agreed, one household at a time, to show up.

5. The East Rand Number
1.4 million Gauteng's population aged 60 and older in 2022 — the largest elderly population, by absolute count, of any province, in a metro region whose aged-care system was sized for a much smaller number

Statistics South Africa recorded approximately 1.4 million Gauteng residents aged 60 and older in 2022, the highest of any province. Ekurhuleni holds close to a fifth of Gauteng's population; proportionately, its own 60-plus population runs into the hundreds of thousands. Nationally that age group grew from 3.6 million (2002) to 6.6 million (2025) — the country's 417 registered facilities for older persons did not grow at anything close to that rate.

6. Closing Reflection
Who Sits Up

The woman who walks into Hospital View most evenings at quarter to seven does not think of herself as filling a policy gap. She is doing a job a family needed, for a wage agreed between them, on a schedule that has held eleven months without a missed night. Multiply her by the drivers who know the dialysis queue and the operators who added a mattress instead of a waiting list, and what emerges is the actual shape of eldercare in a township whose formal system was sized for a population younger, and smaller, than the one it serves now. Tembisa already sits up through the night. The question is whether the city's economy notices.

— Ekurhuleni Underground, 14 August 2026
