## THEME ANALYSIS — ISSUE 32

The theme of the Tambo Memorial Response Line is not a story about ambulances. It is a story about who decides, in the gap between a call for help and a state vehicle's arrival, how a person in Ekurhuleni actually reaches hospital — and about the private, unlicensed transport network that has grown up to answer that question.

**The deep story:** how South Africa's chronic, worsening ambulance shortage — a national shortfall running into the thousands of vehicles against a benchmark the government set decades ago — has produced, around the casualty entrances of Ekurhuleni's major state hospitals, a private response economy: converted panel vans and bakkies fitted for patient transport, off-duty paramedics working private calls on rest days, and informal coordinators who route a waiting family to whichever vehicle can move before the ambulance does.

**Three supporting micro-stories:** the casualty-gate coordinators of Tambo Memorial Hospital in Boksburg, whose knowledge of which private drivers are on call has become as load-bearing as any triage decision; the panel-van operators of the Katlehong-Vosloorus boundary serving Natalspruit Hospital, who have adapted delivery vehicles into cash-rate patient transport; and the off-duty EMS responders of Germiston South near Bertha Gxowa Hospital, who carry their own kit on rest days and answer calls a state radio will never route to them.

**The overlooked place:** the casualty forecourt of Tambo Memorial Hospital on Hospital Road in Plantation, Boksburg — a regional facility serving more than a million people, where state and private response systems arrive at the same gate, though the hospital's own signage acknowledges only one of them.

**The interesting statistic:** South Africa's ambulance fleet is short an estimated 2,221 vehicles against the government's benchmark of one ambulance per 10,000 people — a gap Ekurhuleni's private network absorbs every night, unpaid and uncounted.

### 1. Editorial

**Whichever Vehicle Comes First**

By six on a weekday morning outside Tambo Memorial Hospital's casualty entrance in Plantation, Boksburg, two queues have already formed, though only one of them is marked. Ambulances back up along the demarcated bay nearest the sliding doors. A few metres further along the kerb, a second queue forms without paint or signage: bakkies and panel vans, engines idling, drivers waiting to hand over a patient who never considered the ambulance a realistic option to wait for.

I have been watching this second queue for some weeks, drawn less by the vehicles than by the practiced choreography of the people managing them — a security guard who directs a distressed family toward a specific bakkie rather than the reception desk; a driver who already knows, before anyone tells him, which ward is accepting admissions that hour. None of this is written down anywhere. None of it appears on a hospital organogram. It functions regardless, morning after morning, with a reliability the system beside it has stopped promising.

South Africa's ambulance shortage is, by now, a matter of official record rather than speculation: the national Health Department has acknowledged a shortfall running into the thousands of vehicles, measured against a benchmark it set decades ago and has never met. In Gauteng, barely more than half of the most urgent emergency calls reach their target response time — a figure that understates how the shortage is actually lived, not as a percentage, but as a specific, unbearable wait outside a specific gate for a vehicle that may or may not come.

Into that wait, a private response economy has grown — not a company, not a single service, but a layered informal network of coordinators, drivers, and off-duty clinical staff who have collectively built something the formal system was supposed to provide and, for a meaningful share of Ekurhuleni's population, no longer reliably does. This issue follows that network outward from the casualty gate: who coordinates it, who drives for it, who staffs it on a day off, and what its daily pattern reveals about where a metropolitan health system actually breaks down.

### 2. The Long Essay

**The Capacity That Isn't Deployed**

**Section I — The Observation**

Stand at the Tambo Memorial casualty forecourt on a Saturday night and the two response systems declare themselves through sound before sight. The state ambulance announces itself three streets away — siren rising, blue light sweeping the perimeter wall. The private vehicle arrives without any of that: a bakkie easing into the informal queue at the kerb, headlights cut, engine idling low enough that the family inside barely notice it slowing before it stops. Nothing marks it as anything other than what its papers say it is: a light delivery vehicle, insured and licensed for goods, carrying, on this night, a woman in labour whose family called four numbers before someone answered. The driver knows the hospital's admission routine as well as any paramedic. He has done this run several nights a week for three years. Nobody employs him to. Nobody trained him to. He has simply learned it, the way anyone learns a route driven often enough that the destination stops requiring thought.

**Section II — The Hidden System**

The private response economy around Ekurhuleni's major state hospitals operates through three distinct layers, each solving a different piece of the problem the formal ambulance service has stopped solving reliably on its own.

The first is casualty-gate coordination. At Tambo Memorial Hospital, whose catchment stretches across Boksburg, Benoni, and part of Germiston to more than a million people, an informal group — hospital security staff, community health workers, useful neighbours — has taken on a function no job description names: matching an arriving family with a private driver on call nearby, rather than an indefinite wait for a state vehicle already committed elsewhere. Their knowledge is entirely relational — which drivers answer at three in the morning, which wards are admitting rather than diverting.

The second is the transport layer itself: panel-van and bakkie operators, concentrated along the Katlehong-Vosloorus boundary near Natalspruit Hospital, who have adapted delivery vehicles — a mattress in the load bed, a blanket, occasionally a borrowed oxygen bottle — into functional, unofficial patient transport. They charge per trip, in cash, at rates that vary with distance and hour. None holds an ambulance registration, because South African law requires a costly re-certification before any vehicle can legally carry that designation — a process built for fleet operators, not individuals. What they sell instead is simpler: a vehicle that leaves now, driven by someone who knows the fastest route to whichever hospital is receiving patients that night.

The third layer is clinical rather than logistical: off-duty paramedics, concentrated around Germiston South near Bertha Gxowa Hospital, who take private transport calls on their rest days, using personal vehicles stocked with kit they own themselves. They are the same trained personnel the formal system counts in its own statistics, deployed through a second, informal roster operating entirely outside the shift schedule their employer sets for them.

> *The capacity was never missing from the region. It was simply idle, at the specific hour a specific family needed it.*

**Section III — The Broader Insight**

What the response economy around Ekurhuleni's casualty gates reveals is a distinction the national ambulance-shortage conversation has largely missed: the problem is not, in any simple sense, a shortage of trained people. It is a shortage of deployment. Gauteng's provincial emergency service reports a comparatively high share of its fleet in working order — better, on paper, than several other provinces. What the same reporting also acknowledges, more quietly, is that vehicles and staff are not the same constraint: a service can own working ambulances it cannot fully crew, because a personnel budget and an internship-heavy staffing model do not flex to match where emergencies actually happen.

The private response economy is not, in this light, a parallel workforce filling a skills gap. It is largely the same workforce, redeployed through a second scheduling system the formal one does not control. The paramedic who cannot be rostered onto a state ambulance at two in the morning on his day off can still, as a private individual, answer a call and drive to a casualty gate. The capacity was never missing from the region. It was simply idle, at the hour a family needed it, inside a rostering structure with no mechanism to reach it.

**Section IV — The Antifragile Angle**

The private response economy strengthens, rather than merely persists, at exactly the moments the formal ambulance service comes under the most acute strain. Every vehicle held for months awaiting roadworthy certification, every internship-heavy roster stretched by a personnel shortfall, every budget dispute that delays fleet maintenance produces the same downstream effect: fewer working ambulances reaching casualty gates on time, and more families arriving with no vehicle at all. Each failure becomes, within hours, additional call volume for the coordinators and drivers — volume that deepens their relationships with hospital staff and strengthens the reputation the network depends on for its next call.

This is the mechanism by which the private economy compounds rather than merely survives: it is not competing with the state system for the same patients under stable conditions. It is absorbing precisely the demand the state system sheds whenever conditions worsen, and converting that demand into relationships and repeat business that make the network more capable next time the formal fleet falls short. A staffing shortfall in the provincial EMS budget is, from the casualty gate's perspective, next Tuesday's call volume.

### 3. The Hidden Economy

**Three Operations Nobody Is Watching**

**1. The Gate Coordinators**
TAMBO MEMORIAL HOSPITAL CASUALTY FORECOURT, PLANTATION, BOKSBURG

At the casualty entrance of Tambo Memorial Hospital, an informal group — security staff, community health workers, sometimes just useful neighbours — matches arriving families to private drivers on call nearby, rather than an indefinite wait for a state vehicle already committed elsewhere. Their knowledge is relational: which drivers answer at three in the morning, which wards are admitting rather than diverting. They are paid nothing directly. Their return is standing in a system where the right number to call outweighs any formal qualification.

**2. The Panel Van Operators**
KATLEHONG–VOSLOORUS BOUNDARY / NATALSPRUIT HOSPITAL CORRIDOR

Along the boundary between Katlehong and Vosloorus, where Natalspruit Hospital serves both townships plus Thokoza and Germiston, bakkie and panel-van owners have converted delivery vehicles into functional patient transport: a mattress in the load bed, a blanket, occasionally a borrowed oxygen bottle. None holds the certification South African law requires to register legally as an ambulance — a costly process built for fleet operators, not individuals. What they sell instead is simpler: a vehicle that leaves immediately, cash rate agreed roadside, driven by someone who already knows which hospital is receiving patients that night.

**3. The Rest-Day Responders**
GERMISTON SOUTH / BERTHA GXOWA HOSPITAL PRECINCT

Near Bertha Gxowa Hospital in Germiston South, a smaller, specialised layer operates: off-duty paramedics who take private calls on rest days, using personal vehicles stocked with kit they bought themselves. They are not moonlighting outside their training — they hold the same qualifications the provincial service counts in its own statistics, deployed through a roster entirely outside their employer's shift plan. Clients find them through the same gate coordinators, because a call answered by someone who can assess a patient is worth more than one answered by a driver alone.

### 4. The Map of the Week

**The Tambo Memorial Hospital Casualty Forecourt, Plantation, Boksburg**

*26°13′S  28°15′E*

Two queues, one marked. That is the working geometry of the casualty forecourt at Tambo Memorial Hospital on Hospital Road in Plantation, Boksburg — a regional facility serving Benoni, Boksburg, and part of Germiston to a catchment of more than a million people. In the marked bay, state ambulances back up toward the sliding doors on a citywide dispatch schedule. Along the unmarked kerb beside it, bakkies and panel vans idle in a second queue no hospital signage acknowledges, coordinating with casualty staff through relationships built over years rather than any formal referral.

What you will not find, anywhere on the hospital's frontage, is any indication the second queue exists — no waiting area, no marshal, no line on the tarmac. To stand at this gate at three in the morning, watching a bakkie's headlights cut before it stops beside a family who called four numbers before someone answered, is to understand Ekurhuleni's emergency response system already runs two fleets. Only one appears in any provincial inventory.---

### 5. The East Rand Number

**2,221**

**South Africa's estimated national shortfall in operational ambulances against the government's benchmark of one per 10,000 people — a gap absorbed nightly, uncounted**

South Africa's Department of Health has acknowledged a shortfall of approximately 2,221 ambulances, against a benchmark of one per 10,000 people requiring a fleet of roughly 6,228 vehicles — against the roughly 4,007 currently running. Gauteng fares comparatively well on paper, with a higher share of its fleet operational than most provinces, yet still reaches barely more than half its most urgent calls within target time. The private network around Ekurhuleni's casualty gates absorbs a share of that gap every night, uncounted.

### 6. Closing Reflection

**The Fleet Nobody Registers**

There is a version of the ambulance shortage that lives in a spreadsheet: a fleet size, a staffing ratio, a percentage of calls reached within target. There is another that lives at the casualty gate of Tambo Memorial Hospital at three in the morning, in the decision a coordinator makes about which bakkie to wave forward. Both are accurate. Only one is counted. The private network that grew around Ekurhuleni's hospitals did not set out to replace anything — it answered a phone the formal system, for reasons of budget and backlog, could not reach in time. The question is not whether it exists, but how long a health system can keep declining to see the fleet it depends on.
